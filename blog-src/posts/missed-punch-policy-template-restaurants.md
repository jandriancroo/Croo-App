Every crew member forgets to punch sometimes. The dinner rush hits, the line is three deep, and the first time they think about the clock is when they're already two hours into the shift.

That isn't dishonesty. It's a restaurant. What turns a forgotten punch into a problem is having no rule for what happens next. So the manager guesses, the crew member argues, and payroll gets a number nobody can explain.

A short written policy fixes most of it. Here is the template I'd start from.

## What the policy has to settle

A good missed punch policy answers five questions in plain words:

- Who can fix a missed punch?
- How fast does it need to be reported?
- What proof counts?
- Who approves the edit?
- What happens if it keeps happening?

If your policy answers those five on one page, you're ahead of most operators.

## The template

Copy this and change it to fit your stores. Have your payroll provider or an employment attorney look at it, especially if you operate in a state with its own timekeeping rules.

1. **Report it right away.** If you forget to punch in, punch out, or start or end a break, tell your shift lead before you leave the building. If you realize it later, tell the manager on duty at your next shift.
2. **Say what time it was.** Give the closest honest time you started or stopped, and who saw you working.
3. **A manager makes the edit, never the crew member.** Crew members can't change their own punches. The manager edits the punch and writes one line on why.
4. **The manager checks it against the schedule.** If the edit matches the scheduled shift and someone saw you there, approve it. If it doesn't match, the manager asks before approving.
5. **Edits are logged.** Every edit keeps who made it, when, and the reason. No silent changes.
6. **Pay is never held over a missed punch.** If you worked the time, you're paid for it. The fix is the record, not the paycheck.
7. **Repeat misses get a conversation.** One or two are normal. A pattern gets a private, friendly talk about why, and a plan, before it turns into anything written.

Number six matters most. The Department of Labor's [recordkeeping fact sheet](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/21-flsa-recordkeeping) says any timekeeping method is acceptable as long as it's complete and accurate. The federal regulation on time clocks, [29 CFR 785.48](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-V/subchapter-B/part-785/subpart-C/section-785.48), adds that minor differences between clock records and hours worked are expected, but major discrepancies raise doubt about the records. A written, logged edit process is how you keep the record accurate when the clock misses something.

## Run it on the same day

The edit should happen the day it's noticed, not on payroll day. A missed punch fixed on the spot is a thirty-second correction while everyone remembers. The same punch fixed a week later is a detective job.

That's why the best habit is a quick check at the end of each shift: does everyone who worked have a clean in and out? If not, fix it now. It pairs well with a short [shift handoff note](https://croohq.com/blog/shift-handoff-notes-restaurant-managers/), where the closer mentions any edits the opener should know about.

## Missed punch or buddy punch?

Most missed punches are honest. A few aren't. If the edit requests tend to land on the same person, the same time of day, or the same stretch when no manager is around, look closer. A fair, calm process for that is in [buddy punching and how to stop it](https://croohq.com/blog/buddy-punching/). The policy above helps here too, because every edit has a name and a reason on it.
