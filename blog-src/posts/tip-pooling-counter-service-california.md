Tips are one of the quickest ways a good operator ends up in trouble without meaning to. The rules in California are stricter than many owners expect, and counter-service restaurants sit in a gray area that the guidance wasn't written around.

I'm an operator, not a lawyer, and this is not legal advice. What follows is what the state's own published guidance says, in plain English, followed by the questions I'd take to an employment attorney before changing anything.

## Q: Who owns the tips?

The employees. California Labor Code section 351 says gratuities are the sole property of the employee or employees they were left for. The Labor Commissioner's [tips and gratuities FAQ](https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/FAQ_TipsandGratuities.htm) says employers and their agents can't share in or keep any part of a tip, can't deduct from wages because of tips, and can't use tips as a credit against wages.

## Q: Can I take out the card processing fee?

No. According to that same FAQ, an employer that lets customers tip by card must pay the employee the full amount the customer indicated, without deducting credit card processing fees. The tip also has to be paid no later than the next regular payday after the customer authorized it.

## Q: Can I require a tip pool at all?

Under the Labor Commissioner's guidance, California allows employer-required tip pooling in certain situations. The FAQ says the courts have validated pools among employees who provide "direct table service" or who are in the "chain of service," and the pool has to be fair and reasonable.

## Q: Who can't be in the pool?

Owners, managers, and supervisors. The FAQ says the pool can't be used to compensate them, even if they provide direct service to a customer. The Labor Commissioner's 1998 opinion letter on tip pooling describes managers and supervisors as those with authority to hire, fire, discipline, assign work, schedule shifts, set wages, or adjust grievances. If your shift lead can do several of those, treat that as a question to answer before you let them share in a pool, not after.

## Q: I run a counter-service store. Where do my crew fit?

This is the honest part: the published guidance talks about table service and chain of service, language written with sit-down restaurants in mind. A counter-service store doesn't have tables. Who is in the chain of service when a guest orders at a register, picks up at a window, and sits down on their own?

I'm not going to guess. Whether a cashier, a line cook, a dish team member, or a delivery handoff person fits in your pool is the kind of question that depends on facts, and the guidance says fairness is judged case by case. Bring your actual setup to an employment attorney or to the Labor Commissioner's office before you decide.

## Q: Does a tip jar on the counter count?

Tips left by a patron are covered by the same rule: they belong to the employees they were left for. How you divide a shared jar is the pooling question above. At minimum, write down your approach so it's the same in every store and every shift.

## Q: Do tips count toward the regular rate for overtime?

The FAQ answers this directly: tips voluntarily left by customers aren't considered part of the regular rate of pay for overtime calculations. It also says an employer can't count tips toward the minimum wage in California. Confirm with your payroll provider how your setup handles these.

## What I'd put in writing

Whatever your attorney tells you, I'd write a one-page tip policy that covers:

- Who participates and who doesn't, by role.
- How tips are divided and how often.
- How card tips are paid out and when.
- Who can see the records.
- How a crew member asks a question or raises a concern.

Hand it out at hire and keep a signed copy. Like the [missed punch policy](https://croohq.com/blog/missed-punch-policy-template-restaurants/), a rule everyone has read in advance prevents arguments after the fact.
