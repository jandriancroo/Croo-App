Every operator I know agrees that inventory matters. Almost none of us enjoy doing it. So the real question isn't whether to count. It's how much, how often, and who has the time.

There are two common approaches: a full count of everything, or a spot-check of a short list of items. Each has a place. Here's how I'd compare them for a fast-casual store, and the hybrid I'd start with.

## The full weekly count

A manager or a pair of crew members counts every item on the inventory sheet, on the same day and at the same time each week.

**What it's good for**
- A complete picture of what's on hand, including the slow movers.
- Catching items that quietly pile up or disappear.
- A consistent record week over week.

**What it costs you**
- Time, usually after close or before open, when people are already stretched.
- Fatigue. By item sixty, counts get sloppy. Sloppy counts are worse than no counts, because people trust them.
- Some stores quietly skip it in a busy week, then lose the habit.

## The spot-check count

You pick a short list of items and count only those, often daily or several times a week.

**What it's good for**
- Fast. A short list takes minutes, not an hour.
- Focused on items that move quickly, spoil quickly, or cost the most to run out of.
- Easier to keep up during a rush week.

**What it costs you**
- Blind spots. Items off the list can drift for a long time.
- You depend on picking the right list, and the right list changes with your menu.
- Counts don't tell you the full story unless you also have a full count to anchor to.

## Side by side

| | Full count | Spot-check |
|---|---|---|
| Time per count | Long | Short |
| Coverage | Everything | A chosen list |
| Frequency that's realistic | Weekly or less | Daily or several times weekly |
| Biggest risk | Skipped or rushed | Missing what's off the list |
| Best for | Anchoring your numbers | Catching problems early |

## The hybrid I'd start with

Neither is perfect alone, so I'd combine them:

1. **Count the full sheet once a week**, on the same day, with a manager involved. Keep it under a set time by organizing the sheet in the order of the shelves.
2. **Spot-check a short list on the other days.** Choose items that are expensive, perishable, or that cause real trouble when you run out.
3. **Review the list every month or two.** Swap in items that have been surprising you.
4. **Compare counts to your orders.** If your ordering and your counts drift apart, that's where to look. A [food waste log](https://croohq.com/blog/food-waste-log-vendor-credits-restaurants/) shows whether the difference is spoilage, over-prep, or something else.

I'm not giving you a "right" list here. Your menu and your volume decide it. Start with ten items, and be willing to change them.

## Make the count easy to do right

A few habits that make counts more trustworthy, regardless of which approach you choose:

- **Same order, same units.** A sheet in shelf order with clear units (cases, bags, each) avoids "is that two or twenty?"
- **Count before deliveries are put away**, not after. Or at least be consistent.
- **Have a second person verify** anything that looks off before you adjust it.
- **Record the count when you take it**, not later from memory.
- **Write a short note** for oddities: a damaged case, an open box, a transfer to another store.

The same idea sits behind a good [shift handoff note](https://croohq.com/blog/shift-handoff-notes-restaurant-managers/): capture what you saw while you're looking at it.

## Don't let counts become a one-person job

If only one manager can do the count, it stops happening the week they're out. Teach two people at each store, and write the steps down.
