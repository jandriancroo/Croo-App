A Simple Food Waste Log, and How to Use It to Get Vendor Credits

Jordan Andrian, Founder, CrooHQ
November 24, 2026 · 4 min read

Most stores know they throw away product. Few can say what, how often, or why. That's not a character flaw. It's what happens when the only record is whatever the closer remembers at 11 PM.

A food waste log fixes that. It doesn't have to be fancy. It has to be short enough that a busy crew member will actually fill it in, and specific enough that you can act on it. Here's the version I'd use.

What the log is for

A waste log does three jobs:

  1. It shows patterns. If the same item gets tossed every Monday, your order or prep number is off.
  2. It protects you on vendor problems. A short, dated record of a bad delivery makes a credit request easy to support.
  3. It makes training visible. If most waste is over-portioning or dropped product, that's a coaching conversation instead of a mystery.

It's not for catching people. If crew members think the log is a trap, they'll stop writing things down and you'll know less than before.

The five fields that matter

Keep the entry down to five fields:

  • Date and time: for example, Tue, 2:15 PM.
  • Item and amount: for example, sliced tomatoes, one full pan.
  • Reason: spoiled, over-prepped, dropped, wrong order, or quality problem from delivery.
  • Where it came from: prep, line, delivery, or walk-in.
  • Who logged it: initials.

The "reason" field is the one that pays off. Use a short fixed list so people can pick instead of write. Free text turns into "idk" within a week.

Separate vendor problems from your own waste

This is the habit that makes credits possible. When product arrives bad, don't toss it and move on. Treat it as a different kind of entry:

  • Log it when you find it, with the delivery date and the item.
  • Take a photo with the label or case visible, if your policy allows phones in the back.
  • Keep the packaging or lot information when you can.
  • Tell the manager the same day. Credits are easier to get when the report is fresh.
  • Note the outcome afterward: credited, replaced, denied, or no response.

Every vendor has its own credit process and time limits, so ask yours how it works and write the answer into your log notes. I won't pretend to know your agreement. What I do know is that "it was bad sometime last week" is a harder request than "it was bad Tuesday, here's the photo."

Who logs, and when

Pick an owner. The log works when one person is responsible for each shift:

  • Opening manager reviews the last day's log and checks the walk-in against it.
  • Closer does a final entry sweep before leaving.
  • Anyone on the line can add an entry in the moment.

Keep it where the crew already is. A clipboard that lives in the office will stay empty. A form in the app everyone already opens has a better chance. If your stores use different forms, you won't be able to compare them, which defeats half the purpose. This is the same reasoning behind opening and closing checklists: the record should live where the work happens.

A weekly ten-minute review

Once a week, sit down with the log for ten minutes. Ask:

  • Which three items show up most?
  • Is it the same reason each time?
  • Is one shift or one station driving it?
  • Are any vendor problems still open?
  • What's one change we can try next week?

Change one thing at a time. If you adjust the prep par, the order, and the portioning all at once, you won't know which one helped. Then check again the following week.

Waste prevention starts before the bin

The U.S. EPA's Sustainable Management of Food basics makes the same point: buying only the food you will use is one of the ways to waste less and spend less, and the agency lists prevention through source reduction as a core approach. That matches what I've seen in stores. The cheapest waste to deal with is the waste you never made: tighter prep pars, better first-in-first-out rotation, and counts that match what you actually use. A weekly inventory count helps you see where order and usage drift apart.
