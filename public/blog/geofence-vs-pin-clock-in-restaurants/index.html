Geofenced Clock-In vs. a PIN Pad: How to Choose for Your Restaurant

Jordan Andrian, Founder, CrooHQ
November 19, 2026 · 3 min read

When operators ask me about time clocks, the question is rarely "which brand." It's "should my crew punch from their phones, or on a pad at the store?"

Both can work. Which one fits depends on your stores, your crew, and the problem you're trying to fix. Here's how I'd compare them. I'll be upfront: CrooHQ's clock is a PIN pad that knows the schedule, so I have a point of view. I'll try to be fair about where the other approach fits.

Start with the problem, not the tool

Before picking a method, name what's going wrong:

  • Crews clocking in early or late versus the schedule.
  • Someone punching for a friend.
  • Missing punches at the end of a rush.
  • Remote or off-site staff, such as a delivery or catering crew.
  • Managers cleaning up punches every Monday.

Different problems point to different answers. Most fast-casual stores have the first three, and those are in the building.

Geofenced clock-in on a phone

A geofence limits punching to when a phone is inside a set area around the store.

What it's good for - Staff who aren't at a fixed station, like catering or off-site events. - Stores with no spot for a shared tablet. - Keeping someone from clocking in from the parking lot or home.

What to watch - Location accuracy varies by phone, building, and signal. A walk-in cooler or a strip-mall unit can confuse it. - It depends on personal phones, so you need a clear policy and a backup when a phone dies or someone forgot theirs. - Crew members may not want a work app tracking their location. Be specific about what you do and don't collect, and say it at hire. - It tells you a phone was nearby, not that the right person was holding it.

PIN pad at the store

A shared device near the line or the back door where each person enters their own PIN.

What it's good for - Fast, familiar punching with no personal phone needed. - A visible spot where a manager can see who's punching. - Simple training: everyone learns it in one shift.

What to watch - PINs get shared if you let them. Personal PINs and a written rule keep it honest. - A shared pad can get a line at shift change. Put it where people can use it without blocking the walkway. - It only works at the store, so remote work needs another answer.

What the law actually asks for

Here's the part people overthink. The Department of Labor's recordkeeping fact sheet says employers may use any timekeeping method they choose, as long as it's complete and accurate. Neither method is required. What matters is that the record is accurate and that you can fix errors with a trail, the same thing the missed punch policy is built around.

State rules can add requirements, so check yours.

The question that decides it: does the clock know the schedule?

Whichever method you choose, the bigger difference is whether the clock knows who's supposed to be there. A punch that arrives without the schedule is just a timestamp. A punch that's compared against the schedule can tell a manager that someone is early, late, or not scheduled at all, while they're still standing there.

That's the design behind the CrooHQ time clock: a PIN pad tied to the schedule. It's also why the Monday pile of punches that don't match shrinks, and why buddy punching gets harder to hide.

A simple way to decide

  1. List who punches where. In-store crews, off-site crews, delivery.
  2. Count the stores with a good spot for a pad. Most fast-casual units have one by the back door or line.
  3. Pick one primary method and a written backup for when it fails.
  4. Write the rules down: personal PINs, who edits punches, and what you collect.
  5. Review after two pay periods. Look at missing and edited punches. If the numbers don't improve, change the method or the rule.

If you run three or more stores, pick one approach for all of them wherever you can. Managers rotate. A single method is easier to teach and easier to check, which is a big part of running multiple stores without being everywhere.
