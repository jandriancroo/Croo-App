How to Onboard a New Crew Member in Their First Three Shifts

Jordan Andrian, Founder, CrooHQ
November 18, 2026 · 4 min read

Most people don't quit a restaurant because of the work. They quit because the first week felt like being dropped into traffic.

Nobody told them where to stand. The trainer got pulled to the register. The schedule changed and no one said so. By shift three, they've decided this place is disorganized, and they stop answering texts.

You can't fix everything, but you can fix the first three shifts. Here's the plan I'd run in a fast-casual store.

The idea: one goal per shift

New hires don't need the whole job on day one. They need one clear goal each shift, one person responsible for them, and a clear picture of what "done" looks like.

  • Shift one: belong. Learn the building, the people, and one station.
  • Shift two: repeat. Do that station again with less help, and add a second task.
  • Shift three: contribute. Run part of a rush with a trainer nearby.

That's it. If someone can belong, repeat, and contribute, they've got a real start.

A hypothetical walk-through

This is an example, not a real employee. Call her Priya, a new counter hire at a three-store group.

Shift one, a slower afternoon. Before she arrives, the manager has already put her on the schedule next to a named trainer, Luis, and told Luis she's coming. Priya clocks in with her own PIN, the first thing she's shown, and the manager explains that her PIN is her time record. Luis walks her through the building: where the walk-in is, where the handoff notes live, who to ask. She shadows the counter for two hours, then handles the register with Luis standing next to her. Before she leaves, the manager asks one question: "What was confusing today?" He writes down the answer.

Shift two, a busier evening. Priya does the counter on her own with Luis within earshot. She adds a second task: the closing checklist for the counter, with the steps in front of her. She asks three questions and gets three clear answers.

Shift three, a Friday dinner. Priya works part of the rush. Luis steps back. The manager stops by twice, not to correct her but to check in. She leaves knowing what the next shift looks like because her schedule is already posted.

None of that is complicated. It just doesn't happen by accident.

The manager's pre-shift checklist

Before a new hire's first shift, a manager should be able to say yes to each of these:

  1. They're on the schedule with a start time and a named trainer.
  2. The trainer knows they're coming and has time to train.
  3. Their PIN and clock-in are set up, and they know how to punch.
  4. They have the tasks for their station in front of them, not in someone's head.
  5. Someone will check in with them before they leave.

If you use opening and closing checklists, a new hire's tasks are already written down. That's most of onboarding right there: a trainer who can point at a list instead of remembering everything.

Pay them for training time

Plan on paying new hires for the time they train. The federal regulation on training, 29 CFR 785.27, lets an employer skip counting training only when attendance is outside regular hours, voluntary, unrelated to the job, and involves no productive work. Training for the job someone was just hired to do almost never meets all four. Treat it as work time, and check your state's rules too.

It also sends a message: their time matters from the first hour.

What the schedule tells a new hire

A new hire reads the schedule as a promise. If their second shift changes without notice, or they hear about it in a group chat, you've taught them that this place doesn't keep its word. Put their first three shifts on the schedule in one go, and tell them how changes are communicated. The same thinking is behind getting the schedule out of the group text.

Check in after shift three

Don't stop at three. At the end of shift three, ask the same question you asked in shift one, "what's still confusing?", and fix what you hear. A short written note is enough. If you pass it on, the next manager starts with context. That's the handoff habit applied to people instead of product.
